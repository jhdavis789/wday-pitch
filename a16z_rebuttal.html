a16z's case against Workday — and why we think it's wrong

A point-by-point rebuttal to Schmidt's "Workday's Last Workday?" (Andreessen Horowitz, April 28, 2026).

The bumper sticker

a16z compares a static Workday against moving AI-native startups, compounded over five years. Reverse the framing and the time-to-market gap goes the other way. Workday now ships an entirely new product line — acquisition or build, integration into the tenant, agent layer, partner enablement, GA — in three to six months. An AI-native equivalent needs 18-24 months to build plus another 18-24 months for F500 land-and-expand. Compounded across the next five or six AI products, the static-vs-moving framing is backwards. Workday compounds product velocity inside the install base. The AI-natives compound expensive customer acquisition outside it. The bear thesis requires Workday to be static; the disclosed evidence says it's not.

Below: the seven main points a16z makes, what they're missing, and the data that contradicts each.

1. "AI-natives will rip out enterprise SaaS"

What a16z says. Rippling, Deel, Lattice, Eightfold are growing 50-100% YoY. They'll eat Workday's customer base the way Snowflake ate Teradata.

Why it's wrong. The customer cohorts don't overlap. Workday's revenue is >72% enterprise (3,000+ EE), where the average customer has 23-country payroll, SOX 404 with 7-year audit trails, union benefits across collective-bargaining jurisdictions, and federal-contractor clearances. The AI-native cohort skews sub-1,000-EE. Rippling's flagship public Workday-displacement case is Root Insurance — 1,300 employees, not F500. Combined ARR of Rippling + Deel + Lattice + Eightfold + Gusto + Justworks is roughly 30% of Workday's subscription ARR, and almost none of it overlaps with the F500 base.

The Snowflake analogy fails because Snowflake replaced Teradata at companies that wanted faster analytics on the same data shape. Replacing Workday means rebuilding SOX trails in a new tenant, re-mapping a hundred downstream system integrations, retraining a thousand-person HR team, and absorbing 12-18 months of double-system-running cost. The switching cost isn't UI dissatisfaction — it's physical.

Deel, the most credible mid-market threat by ARR, is actually a Workday Global Payroll Cloud partner as of early 2026. It's not displacing Workday; it's selling through Workday for contractor/EOR payroll where Workday's own coverage is thin.

The disconfirming evidence the bear has to explain away: zero publicly disclosed F500 losses to an AI-native HCM in trailing 18 months. The bear case argues these losses are happening but companies don't announce them. Possible. But the absence of even one named case after eighteen months of bearish narrative is itself a signal.

2. "Workday's UI is terrible — AI will replace it"

What a16z says. Users hate Workday's interface. Conversational AI will eat the front door.

Why it's wrong (sort of). This one is half-right and half-misframed. The UI complaint is real. But Workday is the one shipping the conversational front door.

Sana — acquired September 2025, $1.1B, GA March 17, 2026 — IS the conversational AI replacement for Workday's clickthrough UI. It went from close to GA in four months, the fastest acquisition-to-GA in the company's history. 11,500 customers will route through Sana within 18-24 months. The UI complaint Andreessen has lived on for a decade dies as a topic by FY28.

The a16z piece doesn't engage with the Sana cadence at all — it argues "AI will replace the UI" while Workday is replacing the UI with AI. The bearish framing requires the AI front door to come from an external entrant. The disclosed evidence says it's coming from Workday itself.

3. "Long implementation cycles make Workday vulnerable"

What a16z says. 12-18-month, $20-30M Accenture/Deloitte implementations are a tax that AI-natives don't have. Customers will eventually defect.

Why it's wrong. Implementation length protects Workday more than it threatens it — once a customer is live, they're not switching unless the alternative offers a 5× ROI improvement. None do.

But more importantly: AI is compressing Workday's own implementation cost faster than the AI-natives are eliminating it for themselves. Workday Build + Flowise + Sana + partner-side AI code-gen at Deloitte/Accenture/Kainos compress deployment hours 30-50% over three years. PS revenue in FY26 declined nominally ($728M → $719M) while customer count grew 8%+ — the first evidence the integration-cost-flip is real. A 2022 18-month deployment becomes ~6 months by 2027. That opens the next tier of customer (10K-EE mid-enterprise) who couldn't justify the $20M cutover cost before.

Workday gets a TAM expansion the bear case treats as a TAM threat.

4. "Headcount-clause repricing will compress revenue as AI cuts seats"

What a16z says. Workday charges per-seat. AI compresses seats. Revenue compresses with it.

Why it's wrong on the math, partially right on the timing. The 10-K disclosed the first signs of headcount-clause repricing in the FY25 filing (March 2025): "We have experienced... reduced growth in headcount-level commitments upon renewals of existing customers." That's real. The bear's timing isn't wrong.

But Workday is migrating away from seat-based pricing before the AI-driven seat compression forces the issue. Flex Credits — announced Workday Rising September 2025, ~50 customers live by Feb 2026 — is consumption-based pricing on top of the per-seat base. Management's stated cadence: "second-half FY27 motion, ratably recognized thereafter," i.e., revenue inflection FY28. Realistic trajectory: 8-12% of new bookings consumption-priced by FY28, 22-30% by FY30. That breaks the headcount-clause repricing risk before it compounds materially.

The binary: FAD 2026 (September) sales-force compensation redesign. If the comp model is redesigned to align reps with consumption pricing instead of pure per-seat ACV, the transition accelerates. If not, it stalls and the bear is more right than wrong. The bear case requires the comp redesign to fail; the bull case requires it to ship.

5. "Multi-tenant SaaS isn't suited for AI"

What a16z says. AI requires customer-specific model training. Workday's multi-tenant architecture is the wrong shape.

Why it's wrong. This is the most technical-sounding a16z claim and also the most misframed. Multi-tenant is the right architecture for AI, not the wrong one. Cross-tenant data is what makes the model better. Workday Illuminate already uses anonymized cross-tenant HR and Financials event data to train its agents — 1.7B AI actions in FY26 — and that's a moat AI-natives can't replicate without rebuilding the tenant footprint from scratch. The four-box defensibility test (unique cross-tenant data, workflow done for free, multi-tenant model improvement, regulatory overhead as fixed cost) gives Workday at least three of four boxes on the five Good-AI products. An AI-native at infinite capital can match one box, maybe two — not four simultaneously.

The Contribution Graph — the human-plus-agent attribution layer tying comp, AP, and agent identity in one tenant — is structurally impossible to build outside Workday's footprint. ServiceNow has the workflow but not HR; SAP has AP but not the agent registry; Microsoft has agents but not HR at most enterprises. Only Workday has all of it in one place.

6. "Workday is a static target while startups compound"

What a16z says. Static incumbents lose to moving startups over five years. Workday is the incumbent.

Why it's wrong. The whole framing is reversed. The disclosed evidence says Workday is moving faster than any of its AI-native competitors:

Inside the FDE rail (~4K internal Workday deployment consultants + 15-40K partner staff at Deloitte/Accenture/Kainos/IBM/Cognizant/Alight = 5-7× Palantir's FDE army), Workday's marginal cost to ship a new product to every customer is months, not years. The static-vs-moving framing requires the bear to ignore the disclosed velocity.

7. "Workday's AI revenue is invisible and probably overstated"

What a16z says. Workday discloses AI ARR but it's a vanity number — probably reclassified existing revenue. Hard to verify.

Why it's wrong. Three checks on Workday's $400M+ AI ARR figure:

  1. $100M+ of Q4 FY26 new ACV came specifically from emerging AI products (per Q4 prepared remarks Feb 24, 2026) — that's net-new bookings, not reclassification.
  2. Named customers on Flex Credits as of Feb 2026: Accenture, Nike, Merck. These are paying real per-action consumption charges that didn't exist 12 months ago.
  3. 400+ customers using the 12 agents at GA. Pilots can be reclassified; paid production deployments at 400+ named accounts cannot.

The bear's "vanity number" claim is unfalsifiable in the strict sense — you can always argue any number is overstated until breakout disclosure forces clarity. Which is precisely what's likely to happen by FY28: when AI ARR crosses $1B-1.5B, Workday will likely disclose it as a separate line item, and the bear's "probably overstated" argument has to retire.

What a16z gets right

To be fair to the case: - The historical UI complaint is real. Workday earned the bad reputation. The question is whether Sana retires it; the bear has to bet against the cadence. - The pace of disclosure on AI is uneven. Workday could be clearer about gross AI ARR contribution, retention on AI products, and ACV uplift per agent deployed. The bear's "trust deficit" argument is fair until the FY28 disclosure. - The headcount-clause repricing risk is real and is in the 10-K. The bull case isn't that this risk doesn't exist — it's that the pricing-model transition outruns it. If FAD 2026 doesn't ship the sales-force comp redesign, the bear is more right than wrong. - Sequoia's Cahn is a more thoughtful bear than Schmidt and is conspicuously not making the AI-eats-Workday case. His thesis is that infrastructure capex outran application revenue and implementation fatigue cuts toward incumbents like Workday. The fact that the smartest VC commentator isn't aligned with a16z's framing is itself information.

The trade in one line

a16z compares a static Workday against moving startups and gets a bearish conclusion. The disclosed evidence — Sana cadence, 12 agents shipped, $400M AI ARR, FY25 PS-decline-with-customer-growth, the FDE army at scale — says Workday is the one moving. By FY29, Workday's AI ARR likely exceeds the AI-natives' total ARR combined.

That's not the consensus today. It's what the math forces.
